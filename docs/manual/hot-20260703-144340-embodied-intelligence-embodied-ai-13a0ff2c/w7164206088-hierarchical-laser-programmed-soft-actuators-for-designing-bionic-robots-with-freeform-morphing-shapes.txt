# Hierarchical laser-programmed soft actuators for designing bionic robots with freeform morphing shapes 

Yuhan Guo 1†, Mingguang Han 1†, Weixiong Yang 1, Meihong He 1, Haibin Duan 2,Xilun Ding 1, Sida Luo 1*

Bio- inspired shape- morphing structures, essential for next-generation soft robotics with unprecedented adapt-ability, demand actuators capable of complex and configurable three-dimensional motions. By overcoming tradi-tional stimulus-responsive strategies facing the trade- off between manufacturing simplicity and kinematic sophistication, here, we introduce a spatially differentiated laser-programming technology for digital manufac-turing laser-induced graphene-based soft actuators (LIG-SAs) with freeform morphing capabilities. Via cross-scale control of lasing energy and scribing direction, material heterogeneity and structural hierarchy can be tuned si-multaneously for introducing decoupled electrothermal distribution and stiffness anisotropy, thus encoding LIG-

SAs with four typical motion units: straight bending, directional curling, rigid supporting, and soft connecting. By arbitrarily grouping multimodal morphing units into concretized devices, this approach further empowers free-form design of bionic robots including octopus-like tentacles and inchworm/seal–like crawlers toward multitask locomotion of conformal grasping, path navigation, and obstacle avoidance. This framework bridges digital de-sign with physical intelligence, unlocking previously unidentified avenues of soft robots for creating sophisticated and programmable morphologies. 

INTRODUCTION 

Biological morphology—from octopus tentacle articulation to vine circumnutation—inspires revolutionary developments of shape-

morphing structures, with the capability to reconfigure three-

dimensional (3D) architectures for performing diverse mechanical motions ( 1–3). Benefited from such shape- programming principles, soft robots become one of the most promising intelligent devices with unprecedented multifunctionality and environmental adapt-ability to accomplish multimodal and multiscenario tasks, e.g., for realizing wriggling and leaping movements in robotic insects ( 4–6), swimming poses in robotic fishes ( 7, 8), and flying postures in bionic birds ( 9, 10 ). In such smart devices, the core actuation component with complex, sophisticated, and precise self-morphing behaviors becomes critically important. Accordingly, stimulus-responsive materials, such as hydrogels ( 11 , 12 ), shape-memory polymers ( 13 ), liquid-crystalline elastomers ( 14 ), and nanocomposites ( 15 ), have been basically applied. Although stimulable by fields of electricity, magnetism, light, and hu-midity, these materials with homogeneous structures or compositions could only unlock elementary morphing shapes such as swelling, con-tracting, and uniform bending. Considering apparent constraints of these actuators when con-fronting tasks requiring advanced kinematic coordination and mor-phological reconfiguration, novel strategies need to be proposed for implementing diversified morphing capabilities through program-mable and customizable material/structural design. For a film actu-ator as a critical morphing unit, local programming/modification/ treatment has emerged as the pivotal strategy to transform uniform bending into complex motions such as torsional twisting ( 16 , 17 ), helical coiling ( 18 ), local folding ( 19 , 20 ), and buckling ( 21 ) by ei-ther introducing anisotropic structures or heterogeneous materials. For example, 3D printing, laser engraving, laminating, or mechani-cal gluing processes were applied to alter the actuator with kirigami (22 , 23 ), origami ( 24 , 25 ), lattice ( 26 , 27 ), or thickness-gradient structures ( 28 , 29 ), thereby creating localized stiffness mismatch for guiding specialized morphing. Similarly, inkjet printing ( 30 ), photo-mask patterning ( 31 , 32 ), electrospinning ( 33 , 34 ), or laser etching (35 –37 ) methods were used to spatially allocate distribution, orien-tation, alignment, mixing ratio, and patterning style of active materi-als, thus introducing complex morphing on the basis of asymmetric stimulus responsiveness. Although these local-programming ap-proaches were successfully explored for improving shape-morphing complexity, critical limitations still exist. First, conventionally ap-plied molding, lithography, and printing approaches fundamentally compromise between fabrication resolution and material diversity, for example, photolithography could achieve micrometer-scale pre-cision yet face stringent material-selection standard, while direct ink writing enables multimaterial integration at the expense of reduced resolution and interfacial reliability. Second, although a variety of morphing methodologies have been proposed ( 38 –40 ), integrating multiple, distinct actuation modes into a single, monolithic structure or device often necessitates complex, multistep fabrication process-es that combine disparate materials and techniques. For exam-ple, Wang et al. (41 ) fabricated asymmetric elastoplastic graphene/ piezoelectric actuators with bimodal performance of bending and curling by sequentially introducing fabrication processes of spray-

coating, adsorption condensing, interface transferring, local stack-ing, and hot compression. Certainly, this reliance on heterogeneous integration would compromise manufacturing efficiency and flexi-bility and device scalability and integrity and eventually impede the freedom of shape-morphing programmability. Accordingly, a unique manufacturing strategy for simultaneously supporting high-

precision processing, highly flexible material/structural program-ming, and multimodal shape-morphing control toward successful assembly of soft robotics with various biologically inspired morph-ing structures is highly anticipated.

As a promising solution, laser-induced graphene (LIG) was re-cently emergent for offering a unique one-step laser- writing process to highly efficiently and highly reliably assemble various smart de-vices ranging from sensors ( 42 ), heaters ( 43 ), and electric generators (44 ) to soft actuators ( 45 ). Relying on digitalized programmability for simultaneously tuning processing routes and parameters, the assembled LIG devices reflected great uniqueness for presenting a variety of structures [e.g., surface patterns and freestanding fibers, films, and foams ( 46 )] and a series of functions [e.g., electrical con-ducting, surface wetting, Joule heating, and energy harvesting ( 47 )]. The above features thus facilitated the appearance of pioneering works for proving LIG-enabled soft actuators with multiple morph-ing capabilities by consistently using strategies of either (i) homoge-neous processing of LIG for achieving uniform bending ( 48 , 49 ), (ii) localized processing or directional scribing of LIG for achieving nonuniform bending or curling ( 50 –52 ), or (iii) overall geometric design or multiunit combination of LIG for achieving special mor-phing structures at the expense of bulky sizes or multiple sources (53 –55 ). Although swift design and manufacture could definitely be reflected, current LIG actuators persistently fell in the trade-off be-tween structural integration and morphing complexity, engender-ing limited actuating performances. To best exploit processing and programming uniqueness of LIG, here, we proposed a spatially differentiated laser-programming tech-nology for enabling single-step manufacturing of soft actuators with freeform morphing capabilities through cross-scale control of both material heterogeneity and structural hierarchy. In the basic level, a local-scale process with multimodal tuning of lasing energy and scribing direction together dictates the LIG with decoupled electro-thermal and stiffness anisotropies for exhibiting specific curvature, curling angle, and stiffness, thus encoding four types of basic mo-tions, i.e., straight bending, directional curling, rigid supporting, and soft connecting. In the advanced level, component-scale assembly then programs and bridges all local regions into an actuator unit with distributed multiphysical characteristics toward freeform morphing behaviors. In the premium level, device-scale design is further appli-cable to alter the original shape of actuators or assemble multiple units into a concretized device for assembling diversified bionic soft robots, spanning from octopus-like tentacles for extracting targets in confined spaces or conformally grasping varied targets to inchworm-

and seal-like robots with varied crawling and steering capabilities for path-planning navigation and multitask obstacle avoidance. As brief-ing in Fig. 1, it is highly anticipated that the proposed LIG technology with a cross-leveled designing concept is able to dream and build various plant/insect/animal–inspired soft robots from spatially differ-entiated laser manufacturing, multimodal property control, and con-figurable structure design toward freeform morphing performance. 

> RESULTS

Manufacturing and programming strategies of LIG-SA units toward freeform morphing behaviors 

To study the cross-leveled strategy of LIG-based soft actuators (LIG-

SAs), a U-shaped actuator unit was first applied as the basic pro-grammable structure with strip-like shapes. Figure S1 illustrates the fabrication sequence of LIG-SAs. Briefly, a three-layered Kapton-

LIG-polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) structure was designed for en-abling electrothermally triggered actuation, in which the LIG layer was formed by irradiating a thin film of polyimide (PI) paper (90-μmthickness) through computer-controlled direct laser writing with a specifically programmed pattern to serve as both Joule-heating and mechanical-constraining functions (fig. S2). The Kapton film (50-μmthickness) was taped at the bottom for sealing purpose, and PDMS was spin-coated and cured on top for rendering thermal activeness. Last, the assembled LIG-SA was controlled by a direct current (dc) power source to implement precise electrical-thermal–induced mor-phing behaviors, attributed to a differential thermal-expansion effect from the multilayered structural design (fig. S3). To guarantee accu-rate control of multimodal shape morphing, fig. S4 proves that the resistance of the LIG layer reflects rather low drift effects under both mechanical bending (0.004 %/°) and temperature (0.03 %/°C). To realize freeform morphing, spatially differentiated laser irra-diation is the key to engineer the LIG layer with distributed material heterogeneity and structural hierarchy. Specifically, two types of pro-cessing strategies were raised for designing LIG-SA units, namely, differentiated lasing energy (DLE) and directional laser scribing (DLS). For DLE, site-selective irradiation by applying switchable las-ing energies determines the LIG with different levels of carbonization and resistance ( 56 ), thereby engineering every local region with cer-tain Joule-heating temperature and bending curvature. As demon-strated in Fig. 2A, by choosing lasing conditions between focus (low laser fluence) and defocus (high laser fluence), the focused regions (high resistance) markedly show a higher temperature and bending curvature than the defocused regions (low resistance) when heated by the same current source. Thus, by offering enough temperature difference, the focused region could display knuckle-like motion to connect two neighboring defocused regions with comparatively flat shapes, which is absolutely distinguishable to uniformly bending be-haviors. More convincingly, by assembling varied regions in arbitrary orders, specialized motions of the LIG-SA unit can be customized by allocating knuckle-like structures with varied positions (from left to right), lengths (from 15 to 40 mm), and quantities (from one to five), as shown in both optical and infrared pictures. Compared with DLE, DLS offers another strategy to irradiate LIG with programmable scribing orientations, resulting in anisotropic stiffness and thermal-

induced helical effects. It is believed that DLS effectively creates the microstripe pattern with alternating soft and rigid regions, thus con-straining (or facilitating) the bending motion along (or across) the scribing direction ( 52 , 53 ). As demonstrated in Fig. 2B, by varying the scribing angle from −90° to 90°, all LIG-SA units universally tend to bend with a specific curling angle that is faithfully registered to the scribing direction. Attributed to the dimensional effect of the U-shape with a big aspect ratio, it is also noticed that the 90° unit is almost not deformable, which thus can be used as a rigid supporter. By combining DLE with unique control of local bending and DLS with unique control of curling angle and stiffness, a more cre-ative strategy can be offered to program every local region follow-ing either one of the three morphing types, i.e., regular bending, directional curling, or immobilizing. By successfully applying this spatially programmable and multimodally morphable strategy, free-form morphing shapes can possibly be designed. In Fig. 2C, a series of LIG-SA ribbons were demonstrated with three independent re-gions to be selectively processed under 0°, 45°/ −45°, or 90°. Thus, with arbitrary combinations, a series of specialized morphing shapes were presented. For example, the 90°/0°/90° unit displays a flat-

Precise and quantitative shape-morphing control of LIG-SA units under DLE and DLS strategies 

By raising DLE and DLS strategies, it is then critical to understand the processing mechanisms for quantitatively controlling various morph-ing shapes. First, to avoid undesirable wrinkles, PI paper was selected as the optimized precursor for processing LIG with the best flatness, as compared in Fig. 3A. It was investigated that the PI paper with randomly distributed fibers creates considerable voids to effectively dissipate thermally induced residual stresses ( 57 ). For DLE, the tuning of lasing energy governs the formation of the LIG layer with varied microstructures and electrical properties. Accordingly, by ex-ploiting the Gaussian-distributed laser-energy profile for generating both melted and heat-affected zones (fig. S5), the defocus level ( DL)was applied as the critical parameter (Fig. 3B). When DL = 0, its mi-crostructure (fig. S6) periodically performs the ridge-groove micro-array, which can be precisely controlled by both laser power and scanning velocity for determining the appropriate processing window (6.25 to 28.75 J/cm 2 ). With the increase in DL from 0 to 3.5 mm, figs. S7 to S10 systematically explore the evolution of LIG with an en-hanced spot size (95 to 210 μm), reduced surface roughness (20.1 to 6.5 μm), and tunable porous morphology, chemical composition, and Raman characteristics. With precise control of microstructures, Fig. 3C further summarizes the synergistic modulation of DL and la-ser power for achieving broad-range tuning of sheet resistance from 8 to 261 Ω/sq, thus providing prerequisite conditions for designing Joule heating–determined shape morphing. For DLS, it is noticed that although the laser-scribing angle deter-mines the bending motion with a consistent curling direction, both the number of curling turns and deviation distance are varied from different processing conditions. Comparatively, the deviation distance (Fig. 4B) is changing nonmonotonically. As the scribing angle alters from 90° to 0° (or from −90° to 0°), the deviation distance enlarges first from 0 to 38 mm when the scribing angle is ap-proaching 45° (or −45°), while a further decrease in processing angle introduces an adverse effect to gradually reduce the deviation distance back to 0 mm. Figures S24 and S25 display detailed observations from multiple viewing angles, and fig. S26 shows the 100-cycled durability test. To explain the phenomena, the sheet resistance and bending mod-ulus were compared simultaneously. As shown in Fig. 4C, it is clear that by exerting three-point-bending stress (fig. S27) along the longitu-dinal direction of LIG-SA specimens, the bending modulus notably shows a linear decrease from 103.4 to 33.4 MPa with the change of scribing angle from 90° to 0° (or from −90° to 0°). This scribing angle– dependent modulus confirms the mechanical anisotropy of LIG-SAs with alternating soft-and-stiff microstripe patterns for advising the curling motion toward the least mechanical constraint, i.e., along the cross-direction of scribing. In addition to anisotropic stiffness, electri-cal resistance is another critical factor for determining the bending behavior. By testing along the longitudinal direction, the sheet resis-tance of LIG-SAs reflects a linear increase from 76.3 to 118.2 Ω/sq as the scribing angle changes from 90° to 0° (or from −90° to 0°). It is believed that the ridge-groove microarray introduces highly conduc-tive and low-conductive regions with distinct electron-transport effi-ciency along or across the scribing direction. Accordingly, the coupled electrical performance and mechanical performance together dictate the curling motion with accurate controls of bending turns and devia-tions. For the 0° specimen with the highest resistance (i.e., the high-est actuation temperature) and the least bending constraint, it shows straight bending with the largest bending turns. For specimens scribed from 15° to 75° (or from −15° to −75°) with a moderate re-sistance/temperature and bending constraint, they reflect reduced bending turns but dominant directional-curling effects. For the 90° specimen, it is subjected to the largest bending constraint with the lowest resistance/temperature. Thus, it has no space to curl and is visually immobilized. To better evaluate the combined DLE and DLS protocol with var-ious local-programming strategies, four types of morphing capabili-ties have been categorized, namely, straight bending (e.g., focused lasing and 0° scribing), directional curling (e.g., focused lasing and 45° scribing), rigid supporting (e.g., focused lasing and 90° scribing), and soft connecting (e.g., defocused lasing and 0° scribing). Figure 4D compares these morphing postures before and after actuation. Before actuation, the two 0° specimens are naturally hanging down because of its soft nature, while the 45° and 90° specimens are clearly stiffer. After actuation, both 0°-focused and 45°-focused specimens are showing regulated straight-bending and directional-curling motions, while 0°-defocused and 90°-focused specimens are showing two styles of immobilizations. For the former, the specimen remains reclinate, manifesting its softness and high conductivity, which is immune to electrothermal excitation. For the latter, the specimen becomes even straighter, proving the 90° inward curling for further stiffening of the unit. Beyond these distinct morphing modes, the four actuator types also exhibit pronounced differences in response speed and load ca-pacity (fig. S28), which directly govern their performance in inte-grated structures. To better illuminate these capabilities, Fig. 4E compares a five-region bridge in which three planes are connected by two knuckles. When planes are processed by the 0°-defocused condi-tion, the bridge itself is not standing up. When processed by the 90°-focused condition, as a clear comparison, the 80-mA actuated bridge is able to hold a 6.8-g block that is 9.1 times heavier. Similar-ly, Fig. 4F shows another design in which two edges of the five- region bridge are replaced by the 45°-focused condition for exhibiting an S- shaped morphing structure. To further use the multicapabilities for enabling various complex morphing structures, finite element analyses (FEAs) were performed to accurately predict every local-programming design on the basis of the combined DLE and DLS protocol (fig. S29). Specifically, by ran-domly grouping multiple local regions with independent morphing capabilities, a series of strip and cross-actuators have been pro-grammed to exhibit a variety of special motions with single, double, or multiple knuckle-like components. For example, Fig. 4G uses a straight-bending or directional-curling knuckle to connect two rigid planes for revealing half-fold or angle-fold effects. Figure 4H then introduces two knuckles to connect three planes for revealing more complex shapes, including asymmetric straight-and-angled fold, equidirectional fold, and antidirectional fold. Figure 4I and movie S1 further group four strip units to assemble cross-actuators, in which every tentacle includes one or two knuckles for revealing motions of radial inward crimping, unidirectional rotational pleating, and double-reverse compound folding. Then, single-tentacle robots with three modes of locomotion were presented, including forward curling for swarm collection, distal reflexing for confined-space probing, and heli-cal winding for slender-object extraction. In the first mode, 0°-focused condition was applied to enable straight bending capability. With a high sheet resistance (122 Ω/sq), the tentacle is able to coil at ~720° under 80-mA excitation. By mim-icking the octopus tentacle for tip-initiated food exploration, Fig. 5B and movie S4 demonstrate the complete locomotion of a robot for in-serting into a 3-mm-wide crevice for retrieving an annular item through localized distal buckling that enables interlock hooking. In Fig. 6A, by mimicking the peristaltic movement of inchworms with a discrete anchoring-release mechanism, a type I crawling robot (CR-I) was first developed by designing a dual-strip device with a bilateral archi-tecture for achieving bidirectional locomotion. Thus, by coordinating two independent bending motions (0° processed), the on and off states of excitation sources can be specifically programmed to actuate each strip with an alternating sequence for moving forward or backward (as shown in fig. S33A and text S5). For example, when the right source is on, the right part of CR-I is buckled and anchored, which generates a dragging force to let the left strip move rightward. Then, by shifting the on state to the left source, the left CR-I becomes buckled, while the right strip turns re-laxed. By lastly turning off the left source, the anchored left strip is gradually relaxing while pushing the right strip to migrate forward. Thus, by sequentially providing these buckling-relaxing cycles, the inchworm-inspired peristaltic movement has been achieved and further confirmed by FEA results, as shown in fig. S33B. In addition to CR-I with straight-line movements, a type V crawl-ing robot (CR-V) and type Y crawling robot (CR-Y) were further designed to mimic the terrestrial gaits of seals by using multiple flip-pers. Similar to CR-I, CR-V could achieve leftward and rightward rota-tional locomotion by alternately actuating the two limbs for activat-ing the anchoring-curling-steering sequences. > DISCUSSION

In conclusion, this work addresses the critical demand for soft actua-tors capable of complex and reconfigurable 3D shape morphing in next-generation bioinspired soft robotics. Moving beyond convention-al stimulus-responsive strategies, which often sacrifice kinematic so-phistication for fabrication simplicity, we have developed a spatially differentiated laser-programming technique that enables digital man-ufacturing of LIG-SAs with freeform morphing capabilities. By imple-menting cross-scale control of lasing energy and scribing direction, we simultaneously tailor material heterogeneity and structural hierarchy, thereby introducing a decoupled electrothermal response and stiffness anisotropy. This allows the encoding of four fundamental motion primitives: straight bending, directional curling, rigid supporting, and soft connecting. Arbitrary combination of these multimodal units fa-cilitates the freeform design of integrated bioinspired robots, such as octopus-like tentacles and inchworm- or seal-inspired crawlers, capa-ble of performing multitask locomotion including conformal grasping, path navigation, and obstacle avoidance. This methodology not only overcomes the traditional trade-offs between fabrication flexibility and actuation complexity but also establishes a seamless bridge between digital design and physical intelligence. As such, it opens previously unidentified pathways for creating soft robots with sophisticated, pro-grammable morphologies, substantially advancing the frontiers of adaptive and intelligent robotic systems. 

> MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fabrication of the LIG- SA 

The LIG-SA fabrication process commenced with laminating a 50-μmKapton tape onto 90-μm PI paper (PolyKing, cat. Yilun-P-90) to form a composite substrate. A 10.6-μm- wavelength CO 2 laser sys-tem (Universal Laser Systems DLS 2.3) with a fixed-focus spot di-ameter of ~100 μm was used to selectively graphitize the PI surface following computer-aided design paths. Laser parameters including defocus level (0 to 3.5 mm) and power (0 to 25 W) were program-matically controlled through integrated motion software. Post–laser processing, conductive silver paste (Jingling Co.) was applied to pre-defined electrode regions, forming ohmic contacts with copper wires. A PDMS solution (10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio; SYLGARD 184, Dow Inc.) was spin-coated (1000 rpm, 60 s) onto the graphene-

patterned surface, followed by thermal curing (80°C, 1 hour) to cre-ate a 100-μm active layer atop the 140-μm LIG-Kapton substrate. Final actuator geometries were obtained through precision laser cut-ting along predefined contours. This four-stage process, comprising substrate preparation, laser graphitization, electrode integration, and encapsulation, enabled rapid fabrication of customized actuators within 2 hours and allows batch manufacturing of multiple samples in one substrate. 

Fabrication of the multijoint LIG-SA 

The 85-mm-long parameterized prototype integrates two functional domains: high-resistance joint regions [laser parameters: 2 inch (5.08 cm)/s, 1 W, scribing direction = 0°, DL = 0 mm; red lines in design schematic] and low-resistance connection regions [2 inch (5.08 cm)/s, 2 W, scribing direction = 0°, DL = 2.5 mm; black zones in design schematic]; the remaining fixed parameters of the design are velocity = 2 inch (5.08 cm)/s and pixels per inch = 1000. The assembly of the components is the same as the general processing steps of LIG-SAs. 

Fabrication of various LIG-SA–based soft-robotic devices 

The lantern actuator precursor was fabricated by stacking two prepre-pared hexagonal strip actuators in a face-contact configuration using Kapton tape, with the ends of each strip bonded using additional tape. Both hexagonal strip actuators were connected to one source; upon activation, the lower actuator lifted the upper unit via electrothermal expansion. The flower actuator precursor was formed by folding the prepared array structure into a cylindrical form, with its initial and terminal ends bonded by Kapton tape to achieve a self-standing 3D structure. All grasping robots were adhered to a 10-cm plastic rod using adhesive postfabrication to enable operator-assisted spatial transfer. The circuit-repair functionality of CR-Y was implemented by brushing liquid silver paste onto the bottom surface of the Kapton tape with a soft-bristle brush, followed by natural air-drying to solid-ify the conductive layer. The adhesion-coiling synergistic grasping robots were integrated by attaching 0.05-mm double-sided tape (3M, 55256) to the PDMS side of the robot at equal intervals. 

Construction of the interactive environment of the soft robots 

All targets manipulated by the grasping robots were fabricated from foam with a density of 25 kg m −3, shaped into specific geometries using customized cutting tools. For the ice-melting experiments of CR- Y, ice blocks were prepared by freezing aqueous solutions con-taining 0.1 % (w/v) methylene blue in custom molds. The thermo-chromic properties of methylene blue induced a purple-to-blue transition during melting, which served as a visual phase-change indicator. In the circuit-repair experiments of CR-Y, disconnection sites were modified with copper foil tape to establish robotic con-tact points.

FEA simulations 

The computer-aided engineering module in the commercial software ABAQUS (2022 version, Dassault Systems) was used for FEA to pre-dict the deformation effect of LIG-SAs with different design forms. The temperature distribution field and strain field of the model were calculated by inputting the electric power value by using the electric-

thermal-mechanical coupling calculation method. To simplify the modeling and calculation cost while ensuring the calculation accu-racy, the PI film composed of Kapton, PI paper, and LIG was assigned according to the material properties measured experimentally. The LIG substrate and PDMS were geometrically modeled according to the microstructure morphology in the SEM image to ensure the anisotropic characteristics under different scratching orientations. Given that the materials of LIG-SAs do not involve large deforma-tion, the linear elastic constitutive model is used for the calculation of the substrate and PDMS. The material parameters of PDMS include Young’s modulus of 300 kPa, Poisson’s ratio of 0.45, an electrical con-ductivity of 1 × 10 −7 (S/m), a thermal conductivity of 0.18 W/(m 2·K), and a thermal expansion coefficient of 3.1 × 10 −4. The material pa-rameters of the LIG film include Poisson’s ratio of 0.2, a thermal con-ductivity of 50 W/(m 2·K), and a thermal expansion coefficient of 1 ×

10 −5 . Its electrical conductivity was assigned according to the test results depending on the scratching direction. In the kinematic simulation calculations of friction, the CR-I was modeled as a bilayer 3D deformable part, with its geometry and di-mensions adopted from experimental measurements. The model was meshed with quadratic tetrahedral elements (C3D10H). The ground was defined as a rigid body. A uniform friction coefficient of 0.3 was assigned to the bottom and side contacts between the CR-I and the ground. The variation in frictional force induced by structural defor-mation and movement was computed using a dynamic explicit anal-ysis and normalized by the self-weight of the soft robot.
