Abstract 

Product tables in invoices are obtained autonomously via a deep learning model named ExTTNet. Text information is first extracted from invoice images using Optical Character Recognition (OCR) techniques; both the Tesseract OCR engine and PaddleOCR were evalu-ated to determine the most effective method. Based on comparative analysis, PaddleOCR was selected due to its superior runtime performance, particularly with GPU acceleration, and its deep learning-based feature extraction capabilities. Each OCR token is labelled as a table element or a non-table element, and a multilayer artificial neural network is trained on the enriched feature set. Training was carried out on an Nvidia RTX 3090 graphics card in 62 min. The trained model achieves a macro-averaged F1 score of 0.91 and a class-1 F1 score of 0.90 on a private German invoice dataset, and a macro-averaged F1 score of 0.997 on the public FATURA benchmark. 

Keywords: information extraction; optical character recognition; feature engineering; deep learning; artificial neural network

# 1. Introduction 

In today’s business environment, deep learning technologies are actively utilised across many industry sectors, playing a crucial role in standard company processes such as document processing, recording, and digitisation. The execution of these tasks can incur not only considerable costs but also significant time consumption [ 1]. Manual handling of invoice data—particularly the extraction of itemised product tables—remains a labour-intensive, error-prone bottleneck in accounting workflows. Enterprises that process large volumes of invoices daily must either employ dedicated staff for data entry or rely on costly proprietary software, both of which limit scalability and introduce human errors. This study addresses that bottleneck by introducing ExTTNet, a deep learning model designed to autonomously extract product tables from scanned invoice images. Rather than treating the entire table as a monolithic region, ExTTNet classifies each individual text token as either a table element or a non-table element. This token-level granularity allows partially erroneous predictions to be corrected with minimal manual effort, substantially reducing the workload in downstream accounting processes. The study used a total of 8794 invoices from Germany. The open-source OCR engine PaddleOCR [ 2 ] was employed to extract text tokens together with their spatial and structural features. In the subsequent phase, these

features are expanded through feature engineering techniques, and the enriched feature set is used to train ExTTNet. It is important to note that ExTTNet addresses the binary table-element detection subtask—classifying individual OCR tokens as table or non-table elements—rather than the more complex downstream tasks of structural recognition (parsing rows, columns, and merged cells) or semantic table understanding (fact verification, question answering, and reasoning over cell contents). Recent work on these advanced tasks includes chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning approaches [ 3 ] and graph-based fact verification models [ 4 ]that represent tables as heterogeneous graphs. ExTTNet is deliberately designed as a lightweight, fast preprocessing step that produces clean token-level labels; integrating its output with such higher-level reasoning systems is a natural direction for future work. The novel contributions of this paper are as follows. • A novel deep learning model (ExTTNet) for extracting table texts from invoice images. • A utilisation of feature engineering techniques to improve the accuracy of ExTTNet. • A detailed validation of ExTTNet on both a private dataset and a public benchmark (FATURA). • A comparative analysis of ExTTNet against three alternative network architectures, demonstrating improvements in precision, recall, and F1 score. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 covers preprocessing, OCR comparison, and feature engineering. Section 4 describes the ExTTNet architecture. Section 5 presents the experimental results. Finally, Section 6 presents conclusions and future work. 

# 2. Previous Studies 

2.1. Text-Based Studies 

Within the domain of information extraction from documents, there exists a diverse range of studies and approaches leveraging both rule-based and deep learning–machine learning methodologies. Prominent examples of works featuring predefined rules include Intellix [ 5] and Automatic Indexing [ 6]. In these systems, the information extraction process is governed by carefully crafted templates designed to identify specific patterns within documents. Harit and Bansal [ 7] focus on leveraging header and footer sections of tables in documents, employing a rule-based methodology to extract relevant information. Al-though rule-based approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in specific cases, the field has experienced a significant shift towards deep learning and machine learning techniques. While the primary aim of the CloudScan [ 8 ] study is not to directly obtain tables from invoices, it uses a deep learning algorithm to extract key invoice fields. Due to similar approaches in data labeling and deep learning modelling, this study is included in the literature review. Similarly, there are machine learning-based studies where information is obtained from documents involving OCR-like processes [ 9]. Unlike these studies, ExTTNet specifically targets the prediction of table elements on invoices, with template data on a row basis and relative distance values added as features. Beyond classical detection approaches, recent research has explored graph neural networks (GNNs) and reasoning-based methods for table understanding. Wu et al. [ 4 ]model tables as heterogeneous graphs for table-based fact verification, demonstrating that GNN-based structural representations can capture complex relational information within tables. Yin and Wang [3] integrate a fine-tuned language model with Type-Guided Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompts for scientific table understanding and summarisation. A comprehensive survey of GNN architectures and their integration strategies [ 10 ] provides a taxonomy of approaches applicable to structured document understanding. These advances motivate ExTTNet’s future roadmap, which includes comparison with GNN-based table

models. Additionally, the current study focuses on German (Latin-script) invoices; the multilingual challenges documented in OCR surveys [ 11 ] highlight the importance of evaluating ExTTNet on non-Latin-script invoice collections in future work. 

2.2. Image-Based Studies 

In addition to text-based approaches, image-based methods include object detection and rule-based solutions. Gatos et al. [ 12 ] propose a method where horizontal and vertical lines are predicted and the most suitable table region is identified in a rule-based manner. In the RobusTabNet [ 13 ] project, a deep learning model was developed using direct invoice images. The distinction of ExTTNet is that images are used only in the initial OCR stage; subsequent training relies entirely on extracted text features.

3.1.1. Noise Cleaning The initial preprocessing phase involves noise cleaning. Certain invoices exhibit dot-shaped (salt-and-pepper) noise, while others display vertical line artefacts introduced during image scanning. Eliminating this noise enhances the accuracy of subsequent pre-processing and OCR steps. The OpenCV MedianFilter is applied to each image using a sliding window, effectively suppressing isolated dot-like noise pixels while preserving sharp edges and text boundaries. It should be noted that the median filter is primarily effective against salt-and-pepper noise. Structured artefacts such as continuous vertical or horizontal lines are only partially attenuated; addressing these fully would require dedicated morphological operations (e.g., morphological top-hat filtering for line removal). Residual artefacts from incomplete median filtering can introduce spurious OCR tokens, which would propagate into the 

LineBlockRegex feature with incorrect character-type labels (e.g., a line fragment recognised as ‘ ?’ instead of being absent). In the private dataset, manual inspection of a random sample of 100 invoices revealed that approximately 3–5% of invoices contained residual line artefacts after median filtering; in these cases, an average of 1–2 spurious tokens per invoice were introduced into the OCR output. Although this propagation rate was low for the present text-heavy invoice collection, it could be substantially higher for noisier or lower-resolution scan inputs. A representative failure example is illustrated in Figure 2.

Employing a more aggressive denoising procedure to suppress these thick lines was deliberately avoided, since it would also erode genuine text strokes and degrade the underlying textual content; the milder adaptive median setting was therefore preferred in order to preserve OCR fidelity across the bulk of the data. While this effect was limited in the current dataset, it represents a risk for noisier documents. State-of-the-art deep learning-based denoising methods—such as the Gated Swin Transformer (GCSTormer) proposed by Zhu et al. The algorithm rotates the image in 0.5-degree increments, evaluating H at each step. When H declines over three consecutive iterations, the algorithm halts and adopts the angle with the highest H as the flattest orientation. The deskewed invoice is shown in Figure 6.

A standard alternative for deskewing is the Hough Transform [ 15 ], which detects dominant line orientations in a single global pass and is generally faster for clean, line-rich documents. The iterative heterogeneity-based approach was preferred here because the invoice dataset contains few ruling lines but abundant text; in such documents the Hough Transform can be misled by character strokes, whereas H responds more reliably to over-all text alignment. In terms of computational overhead, the iterative approach requires evaluating H at each 0.5-degree increment; with early stopping after three consecutive decreases, the algorithm typically terminates within 20–30 evaluations (i.e., a search win-dow of ±7.5 degrees), adding approximately 0.3–0.8 s per document on the test hardware. For highly skewed documents (skew > 10 degrees), more iterations are required, increasing processing time to roughly 1.5 s. The Hough Transform would be faster in such cases but was found to be less reliable on text-heavy, low-rule-density invoices. Throughout this study, documents with few images but abundant text were used. For image-intensive documents, a supplementary image-removal step should precede deskewing to ensure higher success.

The attributes listed in Table 3 are not all consumed by the network in the same manner. RawText and TextPattern are auxiliary fields that are used solely to construct the LineBlockRegex descriptor, and are therefore not provided to the model as inputs. The remaining 27 attributes—numerical, binary, and categorical positional features such as BlockWidth , LineCharCount , IsFirstInt , the alignment groups, the ColumnGroup index, the page-quarter indicators, and the proportional margins—are used directly as model inputs, after being normalised to the range [0, 1 ].The LineBlockRegex descriptor is encoded separately. It represents the sequence of character-type labels of the tokens on a line, where each token is assigned one of five symbols ( ?, W, N, F, A). The sequence is taken at a fixed length of eight token positions—lines with fewer tokens are zero-padded and longer lines are truncated—and each position is one-hot encoded over the five-symbol alphabet, yielding 8 × 5 = 40 binary dimensions. Mapping to 67 input nodes. The final input representation is the concatenation of the 27 directly-used attributes and the 40 dimensions produced by the one-hot encoding of LineBlockRegex , giving a total of 27 + 40 = 67 input dimensions. This corresponds exactly to the 67 input nodes shown in Figure 8.

# 4. Our Deep Learning Model (ExTTNet) 

ExTTNet is a fully connected multi-layer perceptron (MLP) comprising eight layers: an input layer (67 nodes), six hidden layers, and a sigmoid output layer (Figure 8). The six hidden layers have the following unit counts (in order): 1024, 1024, 512, 512, 256, and 256. A network with six hidden layers is widely accepted as a deep architecture in the MLP literature [ 18 ]; the term deep learning refers to the use of multiple stacked representation-learning layers rather than to a specific architecture family, and deep MLPs have been successfully applied to tabular classification tasks [19]. Choice of MLP over Transformer-based and GNN architectures. Modern Transformer-based models such as LayoutLM [ 20 ] and Graph Neural Net-works (GNNs) [ 10 ] are powerful alternatives that jointly encode text and layout information end-to-end. However, they require substantially more GPU memory and training data, and their inference latency may not be suitable for real-time accounting pipelines. ExTTNet deliberately adopts a feature-engineering-plus-MLP paradigm: by encoding domain knowl-edge (alignment groups, row-level character-type patterns, page-quarter indicators) as explicit input features, the model achieves competitive performance with far lower compu-tational cost. This design is particularly appropriate for the present moderate-scale dataset and for deployment environments where lightweight inference is a practical requirement. A direct comparison with LayoutLM-style architectures is planned as future work. Activation functions. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function is used in all hidden layers. ReLU introduces non-linearity and enables efficient computation by setting negative pre-activations to zero [ 21 ]. Although ReLU can suffer from the dying neuron problem—where neurons cease to activate when their pre-activation is consistently negative—this effect is most pronounced in very deep networks with sparse or noisy inputs. In ExTTNet, the fea-ture vector is dense and normalised prior to training, which substantially reduces the risk of neuron death. Alternative activations such as Leaky ReLU and ELU were considered; however, preliminary experiments on the validation set did not yield a statistically signif-icant improvement over standard ReLU, and the added complexity was not warranted. Leaky ReLU and ELU achieved validation macro-F1 scores within 0.002 of the ReLU base-line (0.908 and 0.909 respectively), confirming that the improvement was not statistically meaningful. The output layer employs the sigmoid activation function, mapping outputs to [0, 1 ] and facilitating interpretation as a binary class probability. 

# 5. Results 

5.1. Training Setup 

ExTTNet was trained with a learning rate of 0.0001, a batch size of 256, and a maximum of 200 epochs, using the Adam optimiser and binary cross-entropy loss. Early stopping with a patience of 15 epochs on the validation loss was applied to prevent overfitting; training converged at epoch 147. The same optimisation settings (learning rate, batch size, epoch limit, and early stopping criteria) were applied identically to all four models to ensure a fair comparison. The average inference time of ExTTNet on the private test set was 12 ± 3 milliseconds per invoice (mean ± standard deviation over 1758 test invoices), measured on the same Nvidia RTX 3090 hardware used for training. This sub 20 ms latency is well within the requirements of real-time accounting pipelines. Prior to training, feature values were normalised to the range [0, 1 ]. The dataset of 8794 invoices was partitioned into training (70%), testing (20%), and validation (10%)    

> https://doi.org/10.3390/math14132258 Mathematics 2026 ,14 , 2258 11 of 17

subsets via stratified random sampling. A 70/20/10 split was chosen to provide the model with a sufficiently large training corpus while retaining a separate, held-out test set and a validation set for hyperparameter tuning. Given the moderate dataset size, a larger training proportion (e.g., 80%) would reduce the test set to approximately 880 invoices, which was deemed insufficient to assess performance on heterogeneous invoice layouts reliably. k-fold cross-validation was considered but not adopted because the feature engineering pipeline is computationally intensive and the stratified split already produces a representative test partition.

Table 4 provides a statistical summary of the private invoice dataset. The dataset comprises 8794 invoices, from which a total of approximately 1,540,000 OCR tokens were extracted. Table elements (class 1) account for approximately 38% of all tokens, while non-table elements (class 0) account for the remaining 62%; this moderate class imbalance was addressed by stratified sampling during dataset partitioning. 5.3. Evaluation Metrics and F1 Score Clarification 

Performance is quantified using precision, recall, and F1 score, reported both per class and as macro-averaged (unweighted mean over classes) and weighted-averaged (mean weighted by class support) values. The terms Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate 

(WER), referenced in Section 3.2.3, denote standard OCR accuracy metrics defined as the fraction of characters and words, respectively, that differ between the OCR output and the ground-truth transcription. For ExTTNet on the private dataset, the class-1 (table element) F1 score is 0.90 and the macro-averaged F1 score is 0.91, while the class-0 (non-table) F1 score is 0.92 (Tables 5 and 6). The class-1 F1 score is the primary metric of interest because table-element detection is the target task.

5.6. Validation on the Public FATURA Benchmark 

The experiments above were conducted on a private dataset. To address reproducibil-ity concerns, ExTTNet was additionally evaluated on the FATURA dataset [ 23 ], a publicly released collection of 10 , 000 synthetically generated invoice images produced from 50 dis-tinct layout templates (200 images per template), annotated with bounding boxes, text    

> https://doi.org/10.3390/math14132258 Mathematics 2026 ,14 , 2258 14 of 17

content, and field-level class labels. The same OCR, feature engineering, and labelling pipeline described in Section 3 was applied; raw images were not provided to the model. The results are summarised in Table 10. ExTTNet attains an overall accuracy of 0.9977, a macro-averaged F1 of 0.9970, and a weighted-averaged F1 of 0.9977 over 137 ,281 text elements. Critical discussion of FATURA results. The exceptionally high metrics on FATURA warrant careful interpretation. As a syn-thetically generated dataset derived from only 50 layout templates, FATURA exhibits lower structural diversity than real-world invoice collections. The engineered features used by ExTTNet—alignment groups, row character-type patterns, and page-quarter indicators— are highly discriminative for template-driven layouts, where table tokens share very consis-tent positional and typographic properties across documents from the same template. This regularity likely makes the binary classification task more tractable than it would be on fully heterogeneous data. Accordingly, the FATURA results should be interpreted as an upper-bound indicator rather than a direct performance estimate for arbitrary invoice types. A qualitative failure-case analysis was not feasible on FATURA because the overall error rate is below 0.3% and the synthetic templates leave little room for ambiguous cases; a meaningful failure study requires real-world invoices with annotation ambiguities, which will be addressed in future work. # 6. Conclusions and Future Work 

This study demonstrated the autonomous extraction of product tables from invoice images using ExTTNet, a deep MLP trained on OCR-derived and engineered features. On a private dataset of 8794 German invoices, ExTTNet achieves a macro-averaged F1 score of 0.91 and a class-1 F1 score of 0.90, outperforming three alternative architectures with statistical significance ( p < 0.001, McNemar’s test). On the public FATURA benchmark, ExTTNet achieves a macro-averaged F1 of 0.997, confirming that the feature engineering strategy transfers to previously unseen invoice layouts. Several enhancements are planned for future research: • Image-based features in the model. Currently, images are used only during preprocess-ing (noise cleaning and deskewing) and during OCR to extract text tokens. The prepro-cessing steps operate on the raw pixel-level image to improve scan quality; they do not provide semantic visual features to the classifier. In future work, visual information such as line structures, symbols, and barcode configurations—which are not extractable via OCR—will be incorporated directly into ExTTNet as additional input features, enabling the model to leverage information that is currently discarded after the OCR stage. • Transformer-based and GNN architectures. ExTTNet will be compared against LayoutLM-style [ 20 ] and GNN-based models [ 4 ,10 ] to assess the trade-off between model complexity and accuracy gains [24,25]. • Advanced feature selection. The engineered feature pipeline will be refined with feature importance analysis and advanced representation techniques. • Enhanced image preprocessing. Noise cleaning and deskewing will be upgraded using deep learning-based document enhancement methods such as GCSTormer [ 14 ]to provide higher-quality inputs to the OCR stage. • Cross-domain and multilingual evaluation. ExTTNet will be evaluated on additional real-world, publicly available invoice benchmarks—including non-Latin-script collec-tions [ 11 ]—to provide a rigorous assessment of generalisation beyond the German invoice domain.
