Real-time dynamic monitoring of 

# rockfalls with PTZ cameras 

Shang  Huang 1, Zongwang  Yi 1, Xiaoqi  Zhou 2, Xi  Zhang 2, Qingshan  Guo 2, Gang  Xiao 1 &

Pengfei  Chen 1

Rockfall is a prevalent geological hazard threatening lives and infrastructure. Beyond static 

assessment, real-time dynamic monitoring is crucial to capture sudden events, ensuring timely 

warning and risk mitigation. Leveraging low cost, flexible deployment, and high spatiotemporal 

resolution, PTZ (Pan-Tilt-Zoom) cameras combined with deep learning provide a potential solution. 

However, the small size, background similarity, rapid and complex motion of rockfall objects present 

significant challenges for reliable monitoring. To tackle these issues, this paper proposes a rockfall 

detection and tracking framework integrating motion awareness and physical constraints. First, a 

lightweight temporal module is incorporated into YOLOv8 to enhance perception of small and dim 

moving objects. Second, a novel matching strategy based on Euclidean distance and motion direction 

replaces the traditional IoU in ByteTrack to improve tracking stability. Trajectory fragmentation caused 

by missed detections is mitigated via a frame-difference-based completion mechanism. Adaptability to 

unseen environments is further bolstered through a generalization training strategy. A custom dataset 

combining real-world surveillance and simulated rockfalls was constructed. Experiments demonstrate 

superior detection, robust tracking, and strong generalization under complex backgrounds and limited 

frame rates. Deployment on NVIDIA Orin edge platforms facilitates real-time monitoring and supports 

practical PTZ-based early warning for geological hazards. 

Keywords  Rockfall detection, Rockfall tracking, PTZ cameras, Rockfall monitoring, Real-time dynamic 

monitoring 

Rockfalls are frequent in mountainous regions, characterized by sudden onset and sporadic occurrence 1.

They typically originate on steep cliffs with elevation differences of hundreds of meters, where detached rocks 

rapidly convert potential into kinetic energy, generating destructive forces. During descent, rocks may undergo 

a combination of free fall, rebound, leaping, rolling, and sliding, driven by gravity and the morphology of 

the slope 2. Rockfalls not only pose significant threats to human life and infrastructure, but also may act as 

precursors to large-scale landslides or collapses. Therefore, prompt dynamic monitoring of rockfalls is essential 

for facilitating effective early-warning systems and risk management. 

Current rockfall monitoring methods include ground-based sensors and wireless sensor networks (e.g., 

accelerometers, inclinometers, and crack meters), which detect micro-movements with high sensitivity but suffer 

from limited coverage and high maintenance 3,4; remote sensing techniques (e.g., satellite or airborne optical 

and SAR systems), which enable large-area observations but are constrained by low temporal resolution 5–7;

UAV-based photogrammetry and LiDAR, which provide flexible and high-precision 3D modeling of source 

areas, though continuous monitoring is limited by weather and flight endurance 8–10 ; ground-based optical 

and laser systems (e.g., total stations, ground-based InSAR, and TLS), which deliver precise displacement and 

deformation measurements but are restricted by high cost and complex data processing, limiting large-scale or 

long-term deployment 11 ,12 .

In recent years, video-based monitoring has gained attention for its low cost and flexible deployment. Fixed 

cameras allow continuous observation but are limited by static viewpoints and coverage. In contrast, PTZ 

cameras, with pan, tilt, and zoom capabilities, provide high-frequency, fine-grained monitoring over wide areas, 

offering clear benefits for mountainous environments 13 . Combined with deep learning, PTZ systems enable 

real-time dynamic monitoring, supporting intelligent and high-precision early warning for geological hazards. 

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, deep learning-based object detection has made 

remarkable progress. Detection methods are generally classified into two-stage and single-stage algorithms.

Building upon the YOLO framework, various approaches have been developed to enhance the performance 

of video-based rockfall detection. Hu et al. 22  incorporated a hybrid attention module into YOLOX to improve 

small object detection. Wang et al. 23  introduced attention mechanisms and normalized Gaussian Wasserstein 

distance in YOLOv7, reducing miss rates for small objects. Yu et al. 24  combined multi-scale detection heads with 

LSK-Attention and Bi-FPN in YOLOv8, increasing precision while lowering computational costs. Peng et al. 25 

integrated a ghost backbone network into YOLOv8, boosting deployment efficiency on edge devices. Shankar 

et al. 26  paired YOLOv8 with a multi-threaded controller for concurrent video analysis and real-time alerts in 

geological hazard scenarios. 

However, current methods largely depend on appearance modeling from static images, which hampers their 

ability to capture objects in real rockfall scenarios. On one hand, the small size of rockfall objects and their 

similarity in color to the background hinder effective differentiation from the surrounding environment in static 

images. On the other hand, the rapid descent of rocks often results in motion blur, leading to a loss of texture and 

edge details, severely impairing detection and recognition. 

Previous research has demonstrated that motion information is essential for detecting small and dynamic 

objects 27 . However, classical approaches such as background subtraction, optical flow and frame differencing 

each face limitations: background subtraction is sensitive to illumination changes and background motion; 

optical flow is computationally expensive and noise-prone; frame differencing struggles with dynamic 

backgrounds and image degradation, thus producing false positives 28 . To mitigate such errors, Zhang et al. 29 

integrated Gaussian mixture modeling and monocular distance estimation into YOLOv5, while Wang et al. 30 

combined YOLOX with a three-frame difference method. However, these strategies yield only marginal gains, as 

they do not fundamentally improve the model’s ability to capture moving rockfalls. 

Beyond detection, tracking is required to obtain the trajectory, velocity, and duration of rockfalls, which 

supports motion pattern analysis, enhances detection reliability, and provides valuable data for hazard assessment 

and post-event evaluation. Mainstream multi-object tracking (MOT) algorithms are categorized into detection-

based tracking (DBT) and joint detection tracking (JDT) 31 . Compared to JDT, DBT offers greater flexibility and 

real-time performance, making it highly compatible with the requirements of rockfall monitoring. Representative 

DBT methods include SORT 32 , which applies Kalman filtering with Hungarian matching; DeepSORT 33 , which 

incorporates appearance features for robust association; ByteTrack 34 , which leverages low-confidence detections 

to improve object retention; and BoT-SORT 35 , which integrates motion and appearance cues with GIoU distance 

and EMA strategies to enhance accuracy and stability. 

It should be noted that the drastic appearance variations during descent make rockfalls prone to mismatches 

in appearance-based association strategies used in tracking algorithms, while also incurring high computational 

cost. In contrast, motion-based tracking methods demonstrate greater robustness in such scenarios. Within 

this paradigm, most DBT methods employ Intersection over Union (IoU) as the spatial affinity metric and use 

the Hungarian algorithm for data association. Nevertheless, for small and fast-moving rockfalls with complex 

dynamics, especially under limited video frame rates in power-constrained outdoor environments, trajectories 

often fail to overlap with detections, leading to IoU-based matching breakdowns. Moreover, the Hungarian 

algorithm minimizes global cost without considering motion plausibility, reducing tracking continuity and 

stability. 

In summary, the challenges of small object sizes, weak appearances, rapid and complex motions hinder 

reliable rockfall monitoring using PTZ video data. To address these issues, this paper introduces a motion-aware 

detection and physics-constrained tracking framework. The proposed approach not only enhances detection 

accuracy but also achieves consistent trajectory association. The main contributions are summarized as follows: 

(1) A motion-aware detection method is proposed, integrating a lightweight temporal modeling mechanism 

into YOLOv8, which improves the detection capability for small and dim rockfall objects. 

(2) A physics-constrained matching strategy is designed, using Euclidean distance and motion direction as 

matching cues in the ByteTrack framework to enhance tracking robustness for small, fast-moving objects under 

limited frame rates. 

(3) A frame-difference-based completion mechanism is introduced, which integrates motion candidates 

from a three-frame difference method into ByteTrack, repairing trajectories and reducing fragmentation from 

missed detections. 

(4) A generalization training strategy is developed to improve the model’s adaptability to complex and unseen 

scenes, while minimizing false alarm susceptibility. 

(5) A PTZ-based dataset was created, covering both real-world surveillance footage and simulated rockfall 

videos. 

(6) Experimental results reveal that the proposed method significantly improves detection and tracking 

performance with strong cross-scene generalization. When deployed on NVIDIA Orin edge devices, this 

algorithm operated stably at 12 frames per second (fps) on 2 K-resolution video streams, verifying its practicality 

for real-time dynamic rockfall monitoring in field applications.

Motion-aware object detection 

Mainstream object detection methods primarily rely on convolutional operations on static images to capture 

appearance features such as color, texture, and contours. However, in rockfall detection, objects are typically

www.nature.com/scientificreports/ small with blurred textures and indistinct edges, making them difficult to distinguish even for human observers. 

Consequently, appearance-based approaches often perform poorly. PTZ cameras, by contrast, provide continuous 

image sequences that introduce a temporal dimension, allowing motion cues to enhance the perception of weak 

or subtle objects that are barely visible in static frames. This principle aligns with biological visual systems, where 

two parallel pathways process static and dynamic information. Specifically, amacrine cells act as temporal high-

pass filters, emphasizing spatiotemporal differences based on both current and historical inputs, while ganglion 

cells serve as spatial low-pass filters, preserving motion changes and amplifying weak signals 27 ,36 . Inspired by 

this, we hypothesize that even when appearance cues are indistinct, objects can still be effectively detected 

through continuous and salient motion patterns. Thus, incorporating temporal information offers a promising 

direction for addressing the challenges of small and dim object detection. 

In this paper, we propose a motion-aware detection method that leverages consecutive multi-frame inputs 

to integrate motion information with minimal computational overhead. This approach boosts rockfall detection 

performance while maintaining real-time capability for monitoring systems. Concretely, three consecutive RGB 

frames are stacked along the channel dimension to form a 9×H×W multi-frame image cube (three channels 

per frame), which is then fed into the YOLOv8 detection network. This strategy enables lightweight modeling 

of local temporal dynamics without altering the original network architecture, facilitating efficient fusion of 

motion patterns and static appearance. By learning joint features across the three frames, the model can precisely 

localize rockfall objects in the central frame, leading to substantial improvements in detection performance 

under complex conditions. 

Although lacking an explicit temporal modeling module, stacking multiple frames implicitly encodes 

temporal information within the convolutional process. Pixel and color variations of moving objects across 

frames produce distinctive spatiotemporal discrepancies along the channel dimension, which can be captured by 

convolutional kernels as motion cues, while static backgrounds exhibit minimal changes, thereby enhancing the 

saliency of dynamic regions. The resulting 9-channel representation at each spatial location effectively encodes 

short-term RGB evolution, enabling the network to learn temporal correlations between color perturbations and 

spatial displacement without relying on optical flow or 3D convolutions. This implicit mechanism facilitates the 

detection of fast-moving and visually subtle rockfall objects, reducing both false negatives and false positives. 

Moreover, the method is lightweight and easily adaptable to mainstream object detection frameworks through 

a simple modification of the input channel dimension, ensuring suitability for real-world applications on edge 

platforms. 

Physics-constrained object tracking 

In this study, ByteTrack is employed for object tracking. ByteTrack is an efficient multi-object tracking 

algorithm, and its basic workflow is illustrated in Fig.  1. First, the detection results from YOLOv8 at the current 

frame T are divided into high-confidence boxes  Det high  and low-confidence boxes  Det low . Simultaneously, 

historical trajectories are categorized into activated tracks (track-activated, Trk-A) and inactivated tracks (track-

inactivated, Trk-I). Trk-A indicates trajectories that have been consistently matched across multiple frames and 

are thus more reliable, whereas Trk-I refers to newly initialized or unconfirmed tracks with lower stability. For 

Trk-A, Kalman prediction is first applied, followed by the first association round with  Det high . Unmatched 

predicted trajectories from this round  U-Trk 1 then undergo a second round of association with  Det low ,

aiming to prolong stable trajectories and mitigate losses due to temporary detection failures. Next, Trk-I tracks 

are matched with the unmatched high-confidence boxes from the first round  U-Trk 1 in a third round. Only if 

this match is successful is the corresponding trajectory added to the active tracking set, reducing the risk of false 

positives. To satisfy real-time requirements and mitigate mismatches induced by drastic appearance variations 

of rockfalls, the motion-only variant of ByteTrack is adopted for tracking. Accordingly, IoU is used to measure

ByteTrack has shown strong performance in scenarios such as pedestrian and vehicle tracking in traffic 

surveillance and ground-object tracking in UAV imagery, where objects are generally large, move slowly, and 

can be approximated as undergoing uniform linear motion. In contrast, rockfalls exhibit highly variable and 

unpredictable dynamics influenced by terrain and impact events, alternating between rolling with relatively 

stable velocity, free falling with rapidly increasing speed, and bouncing with abrupt directional changes. These 

nonlinear trajectories challenge the linear assumptions of Kalman filtering. The problem is further exacerbated 

on edge devices operating at limited frame rates in resource-constrained field environments. Large inter-frame 

displacements and small object sizes are likely to produce near-zero IoU between predicted and detected boxes, 

leading to trajectory breaks or tracking loss. Moreover, although the Hungarian algorithm ensures globally 

optimal assignment, it ignores the physical plausibility of individual motion. Consequently, in multi-object 

scenarios, detections may be incorrectly associated with distant tracks, causing ID switches and trajectory drift. 

To address these issues, Euclidean distance is introduced as the primary matching criterion to replace IoU. 

However, distance-based matching also has limitations: a low threshold disrupts associations for fast-moving 

objects, whereas a high threshold increases the risk of ID switches from nearby distractors. Thus, a single 

Euclidean distance constraint cannot adequately capture the diverse and uncertain motion states of rockfalls. 

The physical dynamics of rockfalls suggest that rapidly falling rocks tend to maintain a stable inertial direction. 

Motivated by this, we propose a direction-based matching strategy. Specifically, we calculate the angle between 

the motion direction of the current detection and the principal direction of the historical trajectory. This angle 

is used to determine whether the current detection significantly deviates from the original motion path, thereby 

assisting in deciding whether the detection should be associated with this existing trajectory. As illustrated in 

Fig.  2, the black boxes represent the trajectory of historical frames, the red box indicates the current Kalman 

prediction (Pred), and the blue and orange boxes denote two candidate detections in the current frame (  Det 1

and  Det 2, respectively). First, we apply Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to estimate the principal motion 

direction vector of the trajectory (shown as the green arrow). Then, for each candidate detection, we compute 

the directional vector connecting it to the most recent historical box (shown as the blue and orange arrows), 

and calculate the angle between this vector and the trajectory’s principal direction (denoted as  θ 1 and  θ 2). 

By comparing these angles to a predefined motion direction threshold  θ th , we assess whether each candidate 

lies within the extended range of the historical trajectory. Among the candidates satisfying this directional 

consistency constraint, we select the one with the smallest angle deviation as the optimal match for the current 

trajectory. As shown in the figure, although  Det 2, is spatially closer to Pred in terms of Euclidean distance, 

its motion direction deviates significantly from the historical trend. In contrast,  Det 1, while farther in spatial 

terms, better aligns with the historical motion direction and is therefore selected as the final associated detection. 

According to the aforementioned Euclidean distance and motion direction, this paper proposes a physics-

constrained matching strategy that integrates both metrics to accommodate the diversity and uncertainty 

of rockfall motion states. To balance matching accuracy and computational efficiency, a two-stage matching 

process is designed. Specifically, two distance thresholds,  d1 and  d2 ( d1 < d 2), are employed:  d1 facilitates the 

rapid association of stable tracks or slowly moving objects, while  d2 extends the matching tolerance to account 

for potentially fast-moving objects. The motion direction threshold  θ th  is introduced to eliminate candidate 

detections with significant directional deviation, thereby enhancing the discrimination of true rockfall motion 

trends.

Frame-difference-based completion mechanism 

High object velocities and limited video frame rates often result in large positional shift of objects between 

adjacent frames. Missed detections can disrupt trajectory associations, causing tracking interruptions or ID 

switches, especially under occlusions, abrupt environmental changes, or weak object features. To address this, 

we introduce a frame-difference-based completion mechanism using a three-frame difference method as an 

auxiliary detector. Integrated into the tracking framework, this enhances responsiveness to moving objects and 

improves robustness under challenging conditions. 

First, a three-frame difference method is employed to extract motion candidate regions, as illustrated in Fig.  3.

Specifically, inter-frame difference is performed on three consecutive frames (T  − 1, T, T  + 1) to capture motion 

changes in the middle frame. The resulting difference map is first binarized using thresholds to highlight motion 

areas. Then, morphological operations including dilation and erosion are applied to enhance the continuity

computed to generate motion candidate bounding boxes. To remove noise and false responses, candidate boxes 

with areas smaller than a predefined threshold (Area_Thres) are filtered out. Finally, the YOLOv8 detection 

results are incorporated, and a redundant box suppression strategy based on IoU is applied to eliminate regions 

with high overlap with detected boxes. This ensures that only motion candidates likely representing missed 

detections are retained. 

Since frame-difference outputs lack category information, the generated motion candidates may include 

numerous non-rockfall objects. To identify true positives, these candidates are incorporated into ByteTrack and 

validated through association with historical trajectories. A successful match indicates a missed detection in the 

current frame. Due to their susceptibility to false positives, candidates are introduced only in ByteTrack’s second 

stage, alongside low-confidence detections. Successfully matched candidates update current trajectories and are 

retained as supplementary detections, enhancing both tracking robustness and detection recall. 

Generalization training strategy 

The proposed detection method is fully data-driven and relies on large, diverse datasets to achieve robust 

generalization. In practice, rockfalls are rare and sporadic, making the acquisition of sufficient relevant data 

extremely challenging. To overcome this, we constructed a dataset by simulating rockfall events through 

controlled manual rock throws, which effectively reproduce realistic motion patterns. However, this approach is 

constrained by labor costs, safety considerations, and environmental limitations, resulting in limited variability 

in object appearance and scene diversity. Consequently, while the model achieves promising performance in 

familiar environments, recall deteriorates significantly in unseen scenarios, and false positives can occur in 

the presence of unfamiliar disturbances, such as pedestrians absent from the training data. To address these 

challenges, we design a staged generalization training strategy with pre-training and fine-tuning phases, 

enhancing the model’s adaptability to novel environments and its robustness against unexpected interferences. 

In the pre-training phase, the detection model is trained on simulated rockfall data from manual throw 

experiments to establish a base model with adequate coverage. To increase data diversity, task-agnostic 

augmentations such as brightness jitter, scaling, flipping, and mosaic are applied, providing the model with 

foundational capability to detect moving rock objects in unseen environments. 

In the fine-tuning phase, the model is optimized for specific monitoring environments based on the 

deployment location of the PTZ camera, with the goal of improving detection performance in the target area. 

Real-world monitoring videos often contain only background, posing a challenge for environmental adaptation. 

In response, we design a copy-paste synthesis method based on sequences of moving objects. Specifically, we 

extract image slices and positional data of objects from three consecutive frames in rockfall simulation videos. 

These object slices are randomly scaled and pasted onto new background frames. To simulate continuous 

motion, their relative displacement between frames is preserved, as shown in Fig.  4. After pasting, the annotation 

information for the objects in each frame is updated to ensure spatiotemporal consistency. This approach 

generates dynamic rockfall samples without real objects, enhancing generalization in novel environments. 

Compared with domain adaptation methods requiring additional network structures, the copy-paste synthesis 

offers a simpler and more flexible solution. 

To improve robustness against interference, particularly distinguishing pedestrians from rockfalls, we 

introduce a virtual pedestrian synthesis strategy. Static pedestrian masks from the Penn-Fudan Ped dataset 37  are 

used to simulate motion by applying inter-frame displacements across three consecutive frames with physically 

plausible directions and controllable speeds. Pedestrians are restricted to the lower half of the image to reflect 

real-world surveillance scenarios (Fig.  5). Integrated into pre-training, this approach enables the model to learn 

to differentiate rockfalls from human-like disturbances using diverse spatiotemporal interference examples. 

In summary, the method addresses two key challenges in rockfall detection: data scarcity and cross-scene 

generalization. A phased training strategy is employed, comprising pre-training and fine-tuning. During pre-

training, limited rockfall samples are augmented with task-agnostic transformations to establish a foundational 

model with initial generalization. Fine-tuning adapts the model to specific deployment scenarios via synthetic 

sample generation, achieving a transition from generic to customized performance.

Dataset 

To comprehensively evaluate rockfall detection performance, cross-scene generalization ability, and tracking 

stability under complex conditions, a multi-source rockfall dataset was constructed. PTZ cameras were deployed 

across several typical regions in the geologically active mountainous areas and the Three Gorges Reservoir area 

in Chongqing, China to collect monitoring videos. Due to the sudden, unpredictable, and low-frequency nature 

of rockfall events, obtaining sufficient samples through conventional observation is challenging. To address this 

limitation, simulated rockfall experiments were conducted by manually throwing rocks in carefully selected safe 

areas. 

Accordingly, the constructed dataset consists of two types of video data. The first type comprises videos 

from simulated rockfall experiments, including 40 segments (approximately 10,500 frames), in which all 

rockfall instances were manually annotated for supervised training and evaluation. The second type consists 

of surveillance videos collected from real deployment scenarios, including 10 segments (approximately 5,000 

frames) without rockfall events. These videos are used to increase background diversity and to evaluate the false-

alarm robustness of the proposed method. 

The dataset covers 15 monitoring scenes across multiple typical geological environments, including 

mountainous slopes, gullies and debris-flow channels, roadside slopes, and reservoir bank areas. The videos 

were recorded during daytime under diverse weather conditions, including sunny, cloudy, and windy scenarios. 

All recordings were captured using a Dahua PTZ camera at a spatial resolution of 1440  × 2560 pixels (2 K) 

and a frame rate of 25 fps. To simulate the limited computational resources of edge devices deployed in field 

environments, the videos were temporally subsampled to 10 fps for processing. 

The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets at a 6:3:1 ratio, with test scenes excluded from the 

training and validation sets to ensure cross-scene evaluation. The training and validation sets contain a total of 

8,885 annotated rockfall instances, while the test set contains 1,033 instances. 

The monitoring distance between the camera and the slope ranges from approximately 20 m to 100 m. To 

cover large monitoring areas, PTZ cameras are typically operated with a wide field of view, causing rockfall 

objects to appear small in the captured images. As shown in Fig.  6, the object sizes in both the TrainVal (training 

and validation) and test sets are predominantly within 100  × 100 pixels, with most below 50  × 50 pixels, and some

Experimental setup 

Model training was conducted in two stages: pre-training and fine-tuning. Pre-training utilized a subset 

of simulated videos to develop a base model, while fine-tuning adapted the model to target monitoring 

environments to enhance generalization. Throughout training, a generalization strategy incorporating diverse 

data augmentations was introduced to improve model robustness and cross-scene adaptability. Pre-training 

employed task-agnostic augmentations, including brightness jitter, random scaling, flipping, and mosaic, 

along with background diversification using real surveillance images to enrich environmental variation. To 

reduce false detections from pedestrians, 421 static pedestrian cutouts from the Penn-Fudan Ped dataset were 

synthesized into moving sequences and embedded into training samples. During fine-tuning, a copy-paste 

synthesis combined target-domain backgrounds with rockfall sequences from training data to simulate real 

monitoring conditions, complemented by mild augmentations to reduce overfitting. Details of augmentation 

strategies and data sources are summarized in Table  1.

All experiments were performed on a server equipped with NVIDIA TITAN RTX GPUs), running Ubuntu 

20.04, with Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.0, and CUDA 11.7. To further verify its real-time applicability under 

resource-constrained field conditions, the algorithm was deployed on an NVIDIA Orin edge device with Int8 

quantization and multithreading acceleration. 

The object detection model used was YOLOv8s, trained with the SGD optimizer. The initial learning rate 

was set to 0.01, with 300 training epochs, a batch size of 8, and a cosine annealing schedule for learning rate 

adjustment. During inference, the detection confidence threshold was set to 0.3, and the NMS threshold was set 

to 0.5. For object tracking, the ByteTrack framework was adopted, with a tracking confidence threshold of 0.5 to 

distinguish between high- and low-confidence detections. To ensure fair comparisons across different strategies, 

all experiments were conducted using the same network architecture and training configuration. 

Evaluation metrics 

In the rockfall detection experiments, precision (P), recall (R), and F1-score were used as primary evaluation 

metrics. Precision measures the proportion of correctly identified positives, with higher values indicating 

fewer false positives. Recall quantifies the fraction of actual positives correctly detected, with higher values 

indicating fewer misses. The F1-score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, provides a balanced measure 

of performance, particularly under class imbalance.

In rockfall multi-object tracking experiments, three core metrics, IDF1, IDs (identity switches), and MOTA 

(Multiple Object Tracking Accuracy), were used to evaluate performance. IDF1, an identity-based F1-score, 

measures the consistency between predicted and ground-truth trajectories, reflecting the model’s ability to track 

objects continuously. IDs counts identity switches, with lower values indicating better stability. MOTA jointly 

accounts for false positives, false negatives, and identity switches, providing a comprehensive measure of tracking 

accuracy. Together, these metrics assess tracking performance in terms of stability, association consistency, and 

overall accuracy.

Analysis of model complexity and inference efficiency 

To evaluate the practical performance of our methods, we considered three aspects: model parameters (Params), 

computational complexity (FLOPs), and inference speed (FPS). For inference speed, Float32 precision tests 

were conducted on the server, while real-time performance was further assessed on an NVIDIA Orin edge 

platform using Int8 quantization and multi-threaded parallel optimization. All evaluations were performed on 

2 K-resolution images (1440  × 2560), with results summarized in Table  6.

From the detector perspective, the three-frame difference method achieved the highest inference speed 

(81.76 FPS on server) due to its lightweight image-processing workflow, highlighting the efficiency of traditional 

methods in low-resource settings. However, its lack of semantic modeling led to poor robustness under complex 

backgrounds. In contrast, the deep learning-based single-frame YOLOv8 and three-frame YOLOv8 had nearly 

identical complexity (11.13 M / 255.94G vs. 11.14 M / 261.04G). By leveraging temporal cues, the three-frame 

variant significantly improved small and moving object detection accuracy while maintaining comparable 

efficiency (77.52 FPS on server, 12.39 FPS on edge with Int8). When combined with the three-frame difference, 

model parameters remained unchanged, but serial integration reduced server-side speed to 40.05 FPS. On edge 

deployment, multi-threaded parallelism mitigated this overhead, resulting in a minor decrease in FPS from 12.39 

to 11.97. DSF-Net achieved strong accuracy but incurred prohibitive computational cost (1426.24G FLOPs), 

resulting in only 8.45 FPS on server, limiting its real-time applicability in resource-constrained scenarios. 

From the tracker perspective, tracker selection strongly affects system performance. Using three-frame 

YOLOv8 as the detector, server-side Float32 inference measured SORT with the highest frame rate of 50.40 FPS, 

followed by vanilla ByteTrack at 35.97 FPS. Both exceeded the proposed physics-constrained ByteTrack (33.09 

FPS) and its version with the frame-difference completion mechanism (23.52 FPS), due to the low computational 

cost of IoU-based matching. However, IoU-based tracking struggles with fast-moving, small objects. The 

physics-constrained ByteTrack improves tracking accuracy and robustness by leveraging object kinematics, 

but the additional computation reduces system speed. Incorporating the frame-difference mechanism further 

enhances trajectory stability by compensating for missed objects, though at the cost of additional processing 

overhead. When deployed on the edge device, multi-threaded parallel processing enables the tracking stage to be 

overlapped with object detection. Consequently, the tracking time is effectively hidden by the detection process, 

resulting in nearly identical overall frame rates across different trackers. Specifically, SORT, vanilla ByteTrack, 

and physics-constrained ByteTrack reached 12.16, 12.11, and 12.10 FPS, respectively. This demonstrates that, 

despite differing algorithmic complexities, efficient inference and real-time performance can be maintained on 

edge platforms through proper system optimization. 

## Discussion 

Impact of video frame rate on detection performance 

In practical rockfall monitoring, frame rates are constrained by bandwidth, edge-device capacity, outdoor power 

limitations, and long-term storage requirements, making low-frame-rate sampling a common choice. However, 

reduced frame rates cause temporal information loss and disrupt motion continuity, challenging object detection 

in sparse sequences. Systematic evaluation across frame rates is therefore essential to define performance 

boundaries and establish deployment guidelines. At extremely low rates (e.g., 1–3 fps), excessive inter-frame 

displacement renders trajectory association nearly infeasible. Thus, real-time detection on key frames becomes 

essential for maintaining effective monitoring under sparse video conditions. 

To investigate this, comparative experiments were conducted using three-frame YOLOv8 at five frame rates 

(25, 10, 5, 3, and 1 fps). Frame-level metrics become unreliable under varying rates, so event-based metrics are 

employed, treating each complete rockfall process as a detection event. A positive identification in any frame 

constitutes successful detection. This approach aligns with practical scenarios, evaluating the system’s ability to 

promptly respond to rockfall events rather than relying on frame-by-frame accuracy. Event-based evaluation 

typically considers two key metrics:

Limitations and prospects 

Although the proposed method has demonstrated effectiveness in detecting and tracking small rockfalls with 

complex motion under limited frame rates and cluttered backgrounds, several limitations remain. 

First, this study mainly considers relatively simple rockfall motion patterns, such as free fall, rolling, and 

bouncing. In real rockfall events, however, rocks may fragment upon collision with the slope surface or other 

rocks, producing multiple fragments of different sizes. From an object detection and tracking perspective, this 

means that a single object in one frame may split into multiple objects in the next frame. The proposed method 

does not explicitly model such single-to-multiple transitions, making it difficult to accurately resolve object 

association and trajectory linking in fragmentation scenarios. 

In addition, rock impacts often generate dust that can obscure object appearance and lead to false detections. 

During nighttime monitoring, the system relies on infrared illumination; however, infrared light tends to attract 

large numbers of insects, introducing dense motion noise that interferes with rockfall detection. Under adverse 

weather conditions such as rainfall or snowfall, image quality and object visibility may deteriorate substantially, 

while the current system has not yet been optimized for these complex environments. 

Moreover, the lack of large-scale real-world rockfall datasets limits the robustness of the detection model 

under variations in object scale, camera viewpoints, and environmental conditions. The scarcity of such 

data further makes it difficult to accurately model rockfall dynamics, thereby hindering the development of 

algorithms capable of capturing more complex rockfall behaviors. 

Finally, the current method relies on heuristic rules to address trajectory fragmentation caused by bouncing 

motions, while a comprehensive trajectory reconstruction framework for nonlinear and abrupt movements 

remains lacking. 

To address these limitations, future work will focus on several directions. First, the object association 

problem in rock fragmentation scenarios will be investigated, particularly trajectory modeling methods capable 

of handling single-to-multiple object transitions and describing post-fragmentation motion relationships. 

An improved offline trajectory association strategy will also be developed to reconstruct trajectories at low 

computational cost while maintaining temporal ID consistency. In addition, AI-driven data augmentation and 

simulation techniques will be used to generate training samples under challenging environmental conditions, 

such as dust, rainfall, and snowfall, to improve robustness under low visibility. At the system level, future 

work will explore replacing the current infrared-assisted PTZ cameras with low-light PTZ models to reduce 

insect interference and enhance nighttime monitoring. Finally, as monitoring systems expand and monitoring 

periods extend, larger real-world rockfall datasets will be accumulated to improve the cross-scene generalization 

capability of the system and enable more accurate modeling of rockfall dynamics. 

## Conclusions 

This study investigates real-time dynamic monitoring of rockfalls using PTZ video streams. Considering the 

small object size, weak visual appearance, rapid and complex motion of rockfalls, a motion-aware detection 

and physics-constrained tracking framework is proposed. A generalization-oriented training strategy is further 

introduced to improve adaptability to unseen scenarios. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed 

framework achieves high detection accuracy, stable tracking performance, and strong cross-scene robustness, 

even under challenging conditions with complex backgrounds and limited frame rates. When deployed on 

NVIDIA Orin edge devices, the system maintains stable real-time operation at 12 fps with 2 K-resolution inputs. 

From an application perspective, this study validates the feasibility of PTZ camera systems for real-time dynamic 

rockfall monitoring. The developed system enables automatic detection and continuous tracking of rockfall 

motions, supporting timely warning and risk mitigation in slope hazard monitoring. Despite the promising 

performance, this work represents an initial step, and several limitations remain to be addressed in future work 

for more complex real-world scenarios.
